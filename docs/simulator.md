# The simulator, and its limits

**Read this before quoting any accuracy number from this repository.**

Both halves of the experiment are synthetic. The workload is generated by
`amg.workload`, and the models that answer it are generated by
`amg.upstream.simulated`. What is being measured is therefore the **routing
arithmetic** — how much a fitted router's advantage shrinks on held-out traffic,
how far a retry policy amplifies load, what a self-validating cascade can and
cannot recover — and **not** the quality of any real model.

Every accuracy figure in this repository is a property of the table below.

## The table

Accuracy is stated in parts per ten thousand, per difficulty band, per tier.
Written out rather than parameterised, so a reader can see the skill curve
instead of inferring it from a sigmoid's coefficients.

| Tier | d1 | d2 | d3 | d4 | d5 | Input $/M | Output $/M | Relative latency |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `nano` | 97.0% | 92.0% | 76.0% | 41.0% | 19.0% | $0.10 | $0.40 | 1x |
| `mini` | 99.0% | 97.5% | 93.0% | 78.0% | 54.0% | $0.60 | $2.40 | 6x |
| `flagship` | 99.6% | 99.2% | 98.0% | 93.0% | 84.0% | $3.00 | $12.00 | 30x |

Three properties of that table do the work in every finding:

* **the curves separate on hard tasks and converge on easy ones.** At difficulty
  1 the flagship buys 2.6 points for thirty times the price; at difficulty 5 it
  buys 65. A router that cannot tell those two cases apart is not worth having,
  which is what makes the estimator's advantage measurable at all;
* **latency scales with capability.** The flagship is thirty times slower, so
  failing over to it does not merely cost more — it needs thirty times the
  concurrency to serve the same arrival rate. That is the mechanism behind the
  third finding, and a simulator with a flat latency model could not produce it;
* **being wrong is usually undetectable.** `malformed_share` is the fraction of
  wrong answers that are *also* malformed, and it is deliberately low. A
  confident model returns well-formed nonsense. This is the ceiling on what a
  self-validating cascade can recover, and it is the whole of the second
  finding.

## Everything on the decision path is an integer

Nothing in the simulator calls `exp` or `log`, and nothing compares two floats.

That is not fastidiousness. libm's transcendental functions differ by one unit
in the last place between platforms — an earlier project in this series measured
exactly that, one value in 32,768 — and this simulation makes ordering decisions
on latency and threshold decisions on accuracy. A single flipped comparison
changes which upstream won a race, which changes the whole event schedule after
it, and the replay gate in `amg.replay` would then fail on somebody else's
machine for a reason nobody could act on.

So:

* accuracy is an **integer table in parts per ten thousand**, compared against an
  integer draw;
* latency is **integer microseconds**, drawn as the mean of three uniform
  integers — a discrete bell — with an integer-probability heavy-tail branch.
  The tail is what hedging exists for, so it is modelled explicitly rather than
  left to a distribution's shoulder;
* the estimator's weights are **quantised to fixed point** (`WEIGHT_SCALE`), so
  scoring a prompt is integer arithmetic;
* **Python's `hash` is never used.** It is salted per process, so a corpus scored
  in one interpreter and replayed in another would take different branches and
  produce different numbers, with nothing raising. Every draw is keyed on a
  BLAKE2b digest of `(task, upstream, nonce, at_us)`.

The last property is also what makes counterfactual replay possible: because an
upstream's response is a pure function of those four things, any request can be
replayed under a different policy and the answer it *would* have given is
recoverable. Every paired statistic in `reports/evaluation.md` depends on that.

## How far the table sits from real models

The table above is a **stipulation**. To check that its shape is not fantasy,
`scripts/measure-ollama.py` asks a real local model the same generated tasks,
scored by the same parser, through the same `Upstream` protocol.

Measured on one machine: 180 tasks, **36 in every (difficulty, family) cell** so
that no band gets a different family mix from another. Ollama defaults,
`temperature=0`, `format: json`.

*Why one model and not several.* An earlier run measured three, sampling the
first N tasks of each difficulty band. The generated corpus is grouped by
family, so each band got a different family mix, and the result showed a 3B
model scoring 65% at difficulty 1 and 95% at difficulty 2 — a fact about the
sample, not the model. Those runs are not reported here. The sampler now
balances every (difficulty, family) cell; one clean measurement is worth more
than three confounded ones, and reporting the confounded ones would undercut the
only thing this section is for.

| Difficulty | llama3.2:1b | `nano` | `mini` | `flagship` |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 42% | 97% | 99% | 100% |
| 2 | 25% | 92% | 98% | 99% |
| 3 | 17% | 76% | 93% | 98% |
| 4 | 25% | 41% | 78% | 93% |
| 5 | 19% | 19% | 54% | 84% |

Full records, including per-cell counts and latencies, are in
`examples/ollama/`. `scripts/measure-ollama.py` regenerates them.

**This checks the shape of the curve, not its level, and the level is not
close.** `nano` is priced like a hosted small model ($0.10/$0.40 per million);
the largest model that can be pulled without credentials is orders of magnitude
smaller than that. Reading these rows as "the table was validated" would be
wrong. Three things they do say:

* **Being wrong is almost always undetectable, and that is the important
  result.** Across all 180 answers there were **2 malformed**
  and 0 transport errors. Real models return well-formed nonsense, which
  is exactly the assumption the low `malformed_share` encodes and the entire
  basis of the cascade illusion. One caveat cuts against it: these calls set
  `format: "json"`, so the API is helping. Without structured output a cascade
  would catch more; with it — which is what a production gateway would use — the
  illusion gets *worse*, not better.
* **The generated difficulty scale is much weaker for a real small model than
  it is for the simulator.** The measured curve spans **25 points** between its
  best and worst band, and is not even monotone: 42%, 25%, 17%, 25%, 19%. The
  stipulated `nano` spans **78**. So the generator's difficulty parameter
  separates the *stipulated* tiers far more sharply than it separates a real
  model's competence on the same tasks.

  This is the grammar ceiling as a number rather than a disclaimer, and it cuts
  directly at finding 1: an estimator fitted on this corpus is fitted on a
  difficulty axis that a real small model only partly shares. The routing
  arithmetic is still measured correctly — that is what the experiment is about
  — but how much of it would survive contact with real traffic is not something
  this repository can tell you.
* **Nothing flagship-class was measured, and could not be** without credentials.
  Every routing finding turns on the *separation between tiers*, and that
  separation is stipulated. It is the single largest thing this repository
  assumes, which is why it is also on the front page.

The precision is limited: the widest Wilson half-width across these cells is
about **15 points**, so only large differences here mean anything.

Real models are also not deterministic enough to gate on. Ollama at
`temperature=0` is far more stable than at 1.0 but not bit-reproducible across
builds, quantisations or drivers, and `amg.replay` exists to fail loudly when a
replay does not reproduce. Nobody reproducing this repository has the same
models at the same quantisation either, so these numbers are **evidence about
the table, never part of a committed baseline**.

## What this simulator cannot tell you

Stated here rather than left for a reader to discover:

* **nothing about real model quality.** The tiers are stipulated. If the real
  gap between a small and a large model on your traffic is narrower than the
  table's, every routing advantage here shrinks with it;
* **nothing about prompt sensitivity.** A real router's hardest problem is that
  the same task phrased differently gets a different answer. The generated tasks
  have one phrasing each;
* **nothing about tokenisation or context limits.** `estimate_tokens` is four
  characters to a token, stated crudely on purpose so it cannot be mistaken for
  authority. Every cost here is a *relative* comparison between policies over
  identical traffic, and a systematic token bias cancels;
* **nothing about the grammar's ceiling.** The tasks are drawn from a fixed
  generator. A router fitted on them is fitted on that generator's notion of
  difficulty, and the estimator's features — prompt length, digit counts, family
  keywords — are exactly the features that generator makes predictive. Held-out
  *traffic* is measured here; a held-out *grammar* is not, and cannot be.

That last point is the ceiling on the whole evaluation, and it is why the first
finding is stated as "the budget does not generalise" rather than as a claim
about how well routers work in general.
